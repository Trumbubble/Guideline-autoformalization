\section{What each design decision is worth}
\label{sec:necessity}

Every guard in the checker, if removed, produces the same observable: the
system returns a different verdict about a patient. Only the direction differs.
We therefore measure all of them with one procedure and one metric.

\paragraph{Procedure.}
For a rule $R$ and a partial patient record $P$ we compute the verdict of the
full system and of the system with exactly one guard disabled, and record
whether the verdict moved and in which direction. Records are drawn from the
solver, so every value satisfies the domain theory; each record pins a random
subset of the rule's variables and leaves the rest free, since a fully
determined record admits no \textsc{may-violate} and would make the comparison
vacuous. Half of each rule's records are drawn from $\mathcal{D} \wedge \neg
\Phi_R$ --- completions in which the rule really is broken --- because
uniformly sampled records almost never reach the region where two encodings
disagree.\footnote{Under uniform sampling the deontic row measured $0.0\%$, not
because the operator is inert but because no sampled record pinned a
permission's antecedent true and its consequent false. Rates below are
therefore over a sample enriched for disagreement, not over a patient
population.} Runs are deterministic given a seed.

\paragraph{The two directions, stated exactly.}
Verdicts are \textsc{must-violate}, \textsc{certified-compliant} and
\textsc{may-violate} for an obligation, and \textsc{option-taken},
\textsc{option-not-taken}, \textsc{option-available} and
\textsc{option-not-applicable} for a permission. A move is counted when:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
  \item the ablated verdict is \textsc{certified-compliant} and the full
        verdict is not --- a patient cleared where the source was silent, and
        nothing downstream can observe that it happened; or
  \item the ablated verdict is \textsc{must-violate} and the full verdict is
        not --- a false alert, which returns to the first case as soon as a
        clinician disables the rule.
\end{itemize}
For a rule whose full verdict is any \textsc{option-} state, \emph{any}
violation verdict counts as the second case, \textsc{may-violate} included: the
permission lattice has no accusing state, so reporting that a patient may have
deviated is already the harm. Transitions within a lattice
(\textsc{option-available} to \textsc{option-not-taken}) and losses of
certainty in the safe direction (\textsc{must-violate} to
\textsc{may-violate}) are recorded but not counted in either column; the full
transition matrix is in the appendix.

\paragraph{On ground truth.}
The comparison is differential --- the same rules and records under two
configurations --- so no annotation is required to observe that the
configurations disagree. Whether the full system is the correct one is a
separate claim, and its warrant differs by row. For the three-valued verdict it
is a theorem: the three cases are mutually exclusive and exhaustive
(Proposition~1), and a single satisfiability call cannot separate two of them.
For the undefined-predicate rows it is an argument, not a proof: substituting a
value asserts something the source does not state, whereas leaving the symbol
free asserts nothing. For the deontic row it rests on a human judgement --- that
the sentence expresses a permission --- which is exactly the labelling this
system cannot avoid needing.

\subsection{Two samples}

Reported over two rule sets, because each answers a question the other cannot.

The \textbf{generated set} enumerates the expressible space: undefined
predicate in the antecedent, in the consequent, or absent; obligation or
permission; numeric threshold, boolean, time window, or relative change; one
atom, two under conjunction, or two under disjunction. Two instances per cell
over $72$ cells gives $144$ rules, every one of which is admitted by the same
validator and non-degeneracy check the pipeline applies --- no candidate was
rejected. This set says what a guard is worth \emph{when it applies}, with
enough rules per row to be quotable.

The \textbf{existing rules} are the $19$ the system already had, and they are
two different things: $10$ hand-authored directly against the DSL as checker
fixtures, and $9$ produced by the pipeline from open-access guidelines. The
distinction is not cosmetic, because the two halves populate disjoint rows.
No hand-authored fixture carries an undefined predicate or is permissive, so
the substitution rows and the deontic row draw entirely from the $6$ and $2$
pipeline-derived rules respectively. The time-window row is mixed ($3$ fixtures
and $2$ pipeline rules). Every row therefore rests on between $2$ and $19$
rules, and Table~\ref{tab:real} names which, because a rate over $16$ pairs
from $2$ rules and a rate over $382$ pairs from $19$ are not the same kind of
number.

\subsection{Reading the rows}

\paragraph{Substituting a value for an undefined predicate.}
Every guideline in the corpus states predicates it never defines. Both
substitutions are unsafe and they are unsafe in opposite directions, which is
the argument for neither. Position matters and is visible only in the generated
set, where the predicate is placed deliberately
(Table~\ref{tab:holepos}): substituting \textsf{false} for a predicate in the
\emph{antecedent} narrows applicability and clears patients, while the same
substitution in the \emph{consequent} makes the obligation unsatisfiable and
manufactures alerts. Every existing rule carrying an undefined predicate has
it in the antecedent, which is why Table~\ref{tab:real} shows only the first
effect --- and why a system evaluated on those rules alone would not discover
the second.

\paragraph{Deontic force.}
The rate is $80.5\%$ in the sweep and $62.5\%$ on existing rules, but the denominators differ by two orders of magnitude ($2{,}282$ pairs against $16$). Half the generated set is permissive by construction; $2$ of $19$ existing rules are. The
generated figure is the effect when the guard applies; the existing-rule figure is
how often it applies to this corpus. Both are needed, and quoting either alone
misleads. Every one of these is an alert on a patient who declined an option
the source offered.

\paragraph{The occurrence flag.}
A time window lowers to \emph{the event occurred} $\wedge$ \emph{it was within
$n$ days}. Dropping the first conjunct lets a patient who was never tested
satisfy the window, because the solver selects a small day count. All $26$
divergences in the generated set fall on time-window rules, and all move to
compliant. The absolute rate is low because only a quarter of the sweep
contains a window at all; conditioned on those rules it is the difference
between detecting and not detecting the commonest failure of a monitoring
recommendation.

\paragraph{The three-valued verdict.}
The largest effect in both samples, and the only one whose correctness is a
theorem rather than an argument. A single satisfiability call returns the same
answer for ``every completion violates'' and ``some completion violates'', so
it reports both as violations. Roughly two thirds of pairs are affected.

\subsection{Threats to this measurement}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{The samples disagree in magnitude.} Substituting \textsf{false}
        moves $56.0\%$ of applicable existing-rule pairs and $6.3\%$ of applicable
        generated pairs. The directions agree; the magnitudes do not, and we do not claim
        one predicts the other.
  \item \textbf{The generated vocabulary carries plausible ranges; the pipeline
        supplies none.} Existing rules are therefore checked against a weaker
        domain theory, admitting completions no clinician would accept. This is
        a defect in the pipeline, not in the measurement, and it is one reason
        the two samples differ.
  \item \textbf{Records are enriched for disagreement.} Rates are conditional
        on that sampling and are not incidence in a patient population.
  \item \textbf{Rules are weighted unequally.} The unit is the rule--record
        pair, and a rule with more variables admits more distinct partial
        records (from $3$ to $50$, mean $31.7$), so it contributes more pairs.
        A rate is therefore not the fraction of \emph{rules} affected.
  \item \textbf{The deontic row on existing rules rests on $16$ pairs} from
        $2$ permissive rules. We report the rate because the denominator is
        printed beside it; it should not be read as an estimate.
  \item \textbf{Two guards are not measured here.} Merging duplicate concepts
        before the vacuity sweep changes which rules exist rather than what
        verdict they yield; it currently catches nothing in this corpus, the
        collision that motivated it having been fixed upstream. Forcing the
        partial encoding path after a binding failure changes what the
        formalizer emits, and measuring it requires re-running generation with
        the guard disabled.
  \item \textbf{The generated set is synthetic.} It establishes what the
        guards are worth across the expressible space; it says nothing about
        which regions of that space real guidelines occupy.
\end{itemize}
