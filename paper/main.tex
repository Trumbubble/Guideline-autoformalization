\section{What each design decision is worth}
\label{sec:necessity}

Every guard in the checker, if removed, produces the same observable: the
system returns a different verdict about a patient. Only the direction differs.
We therefore measure all of them with one procedure and one metric.

\paragraph{Procedure.}
For a rule $R$ and a partial patient record $P$ we compute the verdict of the
full system and of the system with exactly one guard disabled, and record
whether the verdict moved and in which direction. Records are drawn from the
solver, so every value satisfies the domain theory; each record pins a random
subset of the rule's variables and leaves the rest free, since a fully
determined record admits no \textsc{may-violate} and would make the comparison
vacuous. Half of each rule's records are drawn from $\mathcal{D} \wedge \neg
\Phi_R$ --- completions in which the rule really is broken --- because
uniformly sampled records almost never reach the region where two encodings
disagree.\footnote{Under uniform sampling the deontic row measured $0.0\%$, not
because the operator is inert but because no sampled record pinned a
permission's antecedent true and its consequent false. Rates below are
therefore over a sample enriched for disagreement, not over a patient
population.} Runs are deterministic given a seed; see Appendix~\ref{app:repro}.

\paragraph{The two directions, stated exactly.}
Verdicts are \textsc{must-violate}, \textsc{certified-compliant} and
\textsc{may-violate} for an obligation, and \textsc{option-taken},
\textsc{option-not-taken}, \textsc{option-available} and
\textsc{option-not-applicable} for a permission. A move is counted when:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
  \item the ablated verdict is \textsc{certified-compliant} and the full
        verdict is not --- a patient cleared where the source was silent, and
        nothing downstream can observe that it happened; or
  \item the ablated verdict is \textsc{must-violate} and the full verdict is
        not --- a false alert, which returns to the first case as soon as a
        clinician disables the rule.
\end{itemize}
For a rule whose full verdict is any \textsc{option-} state, \emph{any}
violation verdict counts as the second case, \textsc{may-violate} included: the
permission lattice has no accusing state, so reporting that a patient may have
deviated is already the harm. Transitions within a lattice
(\textsc{option-available} to \textsc{option-not-taken}) and losses of
certainty in the safe direction (\textsc{must-violate} to
\textsc{may-violate}) are recorded but not counted in either column.

\paragraph{On ground truth.}
The comparison is differential --- the same rules and records under two
configurations --- so no annotation is required to observe that the
configurations disagree. Whether the full system is the correct one is a
separate claim, and its warrant differs by row. For the three-valued verdict it
is a theorem: the three cases are mutually exclusive and exhaustive
(the determinacy proposition), and a single satisfiability call cannot
separate two of them.
For the undefined-predicate rows it is an argument, not a proof: substituting a
value asserts something the source does not state, whereas leaving the symbol
free asserts nothing. For the deontic row it rests on a human judgement --- that
the sentence expresses a permission --- which is exactly the labelling this
system cannot avoid needing.

\subsection{The rule set}

The rules are generated, and deliberately so. Measured over the rules the
system happens to have produced, each row would rest on between $2$ and $6$
rules --- not a sample but an accident of which guidelines are open access. We
therefore enumerate the space the language can express: undefined predicate in
the antecedent, in the consequent, or absent; obligation or permission; numeric
threshold, boolean, time window, or relative change; one atom, two under
conjunction, or two under disjunction. Two instances per cell over $72$ cells
gives $144$ rules.

Every generated rule is put through the same \texttt{validate\_rule} and
non-degeneracy check the pipeline applies to a rule a model wrote, and no
candidate was rejected --- so each is a rule the system would have accepted.
The vocabulary is synthetic: a lab with a plausible range, patient facts, an
action a care plan controls, a datable event, an undefined predicate. This
tells us what a guard is worth \emph{when it applies}. It does not tell us
which regions of that space real guidelines occupy, and we make no claim that
it does.

\subsection{Reading the rows}

\paragraph{Substituting a value for an undefined predicate.}
Every guideline in the corpus states predicates it never defines. Both
substitutions are unsafe and they are unsafe in opposite directions, which is
the argument for neither. Position determines which: substituting
\textsf{false} for a predicate in the \emph{antecedent} narrows applicability
and clears patients, while the same substitution in the \emph{consequent} makes
the obligation unsatisfiable and manufactures alerts
(Table~\ref{tab:holepos}), and the two substitutions are mirror images. Both
columns of Table~\ref{tab:generated} understate the effect, because half the
generated rules are permissive and a permission cannot move to
\textsc{certified-compliant} at all. Restricted to obligations, substituting
\textsf{false} clears $9.6\%$ of pairs and accuses $7.9\%$; substituting
\textsf{true} clears $5.3\%$ and accuses $10.2\%$.

\paragraph{The two zeros.}
Neither is an empty cell of the sample. A permission's full verdict is an
\textsc{option-} state, and the counting rule treats only a violation verdict
against that baseline as a move, so a permission can never be recorded as
moving to \textsc{certified-compliant}; and the single-query check returns only
\textsc{must-violate} or \textsc{certified-compliant}, and it returns the
latter exactly when the full system does. Both zeros follow from the
definitions rather than from what was sampled.

\paragraph{Deontic force.}
The largest effect measured: once a rule is permissive, dropping the operator
changes the verdict for four pairs in five ($80.0\%$). Every one is an alert on a patient
who declined an option the source offered.

\paragraph{The occurrence flag.}
A time window lowers to \emph{the event occurred} $\wedge$ \emph{it was within
$n$ days}. Dropping the first conjunct lets a patient who was never tested
satisfy the window, because the solver selects a small day count. All $120$
divergences move to compliant, and all fall on time-window rules. The flag is
decisive only when the rest of the record leaves the window in question, so the
rate is a floor rather than a frequency; every instance is a patient who was
never tested being reported as compliant.

\paragraph{The three-valued verdict.}
The only row whose correctness is a theorem rather than an argument. A single
satisfiability call returns the same answer for ``every completion violates''
and ``some completion violates'', so it reports both as violations. This row
uses the rule's own violation condition, so it does not also drop the deontic
operator; measured against $\neg\Phi_R$ instead it double-counts the deontic row above
it, because every permission then reads as violable.

\subsection{What an undetermined verdict rests on}
\label{sec:may}

A verdict of \textsc{may-violate} is only useful if the reader is told what it
turns on. The witness itself is not that: it assigns a value to every unstated
variable, most of them incidental. \texttt{minimal\_counterexample\_core}
returns instead the smallest set of those values that on its own forces the
violation, by deletion-based extraction --- drop one assignment, keep the drop
if the remainder still cannot be made compliant. Because the check leaves every
non-core variable unconstrained, unsatisfiability quantifies over all their
completions, so the core's values force a violation \emph{whatever the
unmeasured variables turn out to be}. The result is minimal, not minimum: an
irreducible set, not provably the smallest, so sizes are an upper bound.

Of $5{,}764$ records, $1{,}783$ returned \textsc{may-violate}. The core is
smaller than the set of unstated variables ($1.39$ against $2.42$ on average),
and the reduction grows as the record thins, because the core is bounded by the
rule's structure --- the number of atoms that must be forced --- and not by how
much the record omits.

\paragraph{Not every explanation names something that can be measured.}
$1{,}165$ of the $1{,}783$ undetermined verdicts ($65.3\%$) rest on a single
variable, but $161$ of those name an \emph{undefined predicate} --- a phrase a
source states and never defines --- which no measurement resolves, because
there is nothing to measure. Splitting them: $1{,}004$ ($56.3\%$) turn on a
single bound concept, $161$ ($9.0\%$) on a single undefined predicate, and
across all core sizes $353$ ($19.8\%$) involve an undefined predicate
somewhere. A system
that substituted a value for those predicates would return a definite verdict
in every one of those cases, and would be asserting something the source does
not say each time.

These proportions are a property of the rule set, not a finding about
guidelines: undefined predicates were placed in two thirds of the generated
rules by construction, so how often they end up carrying a verdict follows from
that design. What the measurement does establish is that the distinction is
\emph{visible} --- the system can say which undetermined verdicts are waiting on
a measurement and which are waiting on a definition the source never gave.

\paragraph{Supplying the core resolves the verdict.}
Pinning the values the core named and re-reading the verdict gives
\textsc{must-violate} in $1{,}801$ of $1{,}801$ cases. This is true by
construction --- the core is extracted precisely as a set whose values make
compliance unsatisfiable --- so it is reported as a check on the implementation
rather than as a result. It rules out a minimiser that returns cores which do
not do what they claim, which is worth ruling out.

\paragraph{Would measuring the named variable settle it?}
That a core is a single variable does not mean measuring it answers the
question. The core is sufficient for \emph{one} outcome: those values force a
violation, while a different value may leave the verdict open. The claim a
clinical reader takes from ``measure $X$'' is stronger --- that \emph{every}
value of $X$ determines the verdict --- and it is decidable without enumerating
values. A verdict is undetermined at $x$ exactly when some completion complies
and some completion violates, so take two copies of the remaining free
variables, share $X$ between them, and ask whether both can hold at once:
\[
  \mathrm{SAT}\bigl[\;
    (\mathcal{D} \wedge P \wedge \Phi_R)[\bar{R} \mapsto \bar{R}_1]
    \;\wedge\;
    (\mathcal{D} \wedge P \wedge \neg\Phi_R)[\bar{R} \mapsto \bar{R}_2]
  \;\bigr]
\]
Satisfiable means some value of $X$ still leaves the verdict open; unsatisfiable
means measuring $X$ resolves the case whatever comes back.

Of the $1{,}183$ single-variable cores, measuring the named variable determines
the verdict in $840$ ($71.0\%$), which is $46.6\%$ of all undetermined verdicts.
In the other $343$ ($29.0\%$) some value of it leaves the case open. The naive
reading --- counting single-variable cores as resolvable measurements --- would
have claimed $65.7\%$ and overstated by nearly a third.

Which of the two holds is a property of the record, not of the rule. For
$(\textsf{potassium} < 5.0 \vee \textsf{has\_ckd}) \rightarrow \textsf{on\_acei}$
one record yields a core of \textsf{potassium} that does not settle the case ---
a reading at or above the threshold leaves \textsf{has\_ckd} free to make the
antecedent true anyway --- while another record on the same rule yields a core of
\textsf{on\_acei} that does, because the antecedent is already pinned true and
the consequent is the only thing left to decide.

\paragraph{The core is not a measurement plan.}
It is a sufficient condition for one outcome. A reader who measures the named
variable and finds a different value has learned nothing conclusive: the verdict
may remain undetermined, and a variable outside the core may then decide it.
Answering ``what should be measured next'' requires a sweep over which
observations most reduce uncertainty, which we do not attempt. Nor is the core
unique --- it is extracted from one witness, and another witness may give a
disjoint set that forces a violation by a different route.

\subsection{Robustness and threats}

The generated vocabulary assigns every numeric variable a plausible
physiological range; the pipeline currently supplies none, so a rule it
produces is checked against a weaker domain theory. Re-running the sweep with
the ranges stripped moves every rate by less than half a point (substituting
\textsf{false}: $8.8\%$ to $8.7\%$; deontic: $80.0\%$ to $79.4\%$; single query:
$30.8\%$ to $30.6\%$), so the result does not rest on that difference.

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{The rules are synthetic.} This establishes what each guard is
        worth across the expressible space. It says nothing about which regions
        of that space real guidelines occupy, and the sweep is uniform over
        cells where real material certainly is not. Measured on the $19$ rules
        the system has produced, the directions of harm are the same but the
        magnitudes differ substantially, and we were not able to isolate which
        property of the rules accounts for that; we therefore report the
        generated set alone rather than a comparison we cannot explain.
  \item \textbf{The sampler needed correcting, and the correction moved every
        row.} Z3 is deterministic about unconstrained symbols --- an
        unconstrained boolean returns false in $40$ of $40$ fresh solvers
        whatever the seed --- so drawing records straight from a model never
        pinned a boolean true unless the formula forced it. That is a
        systematic bias, and it removed entire verdict transitions from the
        measurement: substituting \textsf{true} for an undefined predicate in a
        \emph{consequent} should sometimes clear a patient, and it was recorded
        as never doing so, because the records that show it pin an action
        variable true and the sampler could not produce one. Records are now
        pushed away from Z3's defaults with probability one half, each push
        kept only while the constraints stay satisfiable. Every rate in this
        section changed: the occurrence row moved from $1.4\%$ to $7.0\%$.
  \item \textbf{Records are enriched for disagreement.} Rates are conditional
        on that sampling and are not incidence in a patient population.
  \item \textbf{Rules are weighted unequally.} The unit is the rule--record
        pair, and a rule with more variables admits more distinct partial
        records ($3$ to $50$, mean $31.7$), so it contributes more pairs. A
        rate is not the fraction of \emph{rules} affected.
  \item \textbf{Two guards are not measured here.} Merging duplicate concepts
        before the vacuity sweep changes which rules exist rather than what
        verdict they yield, and it catches nothing in the current corpus, the
        collision that motivated it having been fixed upstream. Forcing the
        partial encoding path after a binding failure changes what the
        formalizer emits, and measuring it requires re-running generation with
        the guard disabled.
\end{itemize}

\section{Reproducing the measurements}
\label{app:repro}

\paragraph{Commands.}
Every number in Section~\ref{sec:necessity} comes from one of four scripts in
\texttt{Part B/tests}. None makes a model call; none needs an API key.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
  \toprule
  Produces & Command \\
  \midrule
  Tables \ref{tab:generated}, \ref{tab:holepos}
    & \texttt{ablation.py -{}-generated -{}-plans 50 -{}-seed 0} \\
  Core sizes, \S\ref{sec:may}
    & \texttt{explanation\_size.py -{}-plans 50 -{}-seed 0} \\
  Both \textsc{may} experiments, \S\ref{sec:may}
    & \texttt{may\_actionability.py -{}-plans 50 -{}-seed 0} \\
  The rule set itself
    & \texttt{generated\_rules.py -{}-list} \\
  \addlinespace
  The propositions stated in \texttt{logic.tex}
    & \texttt{propositions.py} \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Each writes a CSV of per-verdict rows beside a short summary, both in the
artefact; the summaries are what the tables quote. Two variants are runnable and
not reported: \texttt{ablation.py} without \texttt{-{}-generated} runs over the
rules the pipeline itself produced, and \texttt{-{}-no-ranges} strips the
plausible ranges from the generated vocabulary.

\paragraph{Environment.}
Python 3.14.2, \texttt{z3-solver} 5.1.0, macOS 15.5 (arm64). The commit is
recorded in the artefact. Z3 is the only dependency of these two scripts.

\paragraph{Determinism.}
Runs are reproducible byte for byte given the seed: three separate processes
produce identical CSVs. This required one fix worth recording, because it is
the kind of thing that silently invalidates a reported number. A rule's
variable map is built from a Python \emph{set}, whose iteration order varies
between processes under hash randomisation; the record sampler drew from that
order, so the same seed gave different counts run to run (for one row, $103$
against $104$). The sampler now sorts the names. Anyone reproducing a
solver-driven measurement in Python should check this specifically --- the
symptom is a stable seed with unstable output, and it is invisible within a
single process.

A second limit bounds that guarantee. Each script is reproducible \emph{in its
own process}, but calling the record sampler twice inside one process is not:
the same seed gave $4{,}568$ records and then $4{,}570$. The cause is below our
code --- Z3 carries global state that constructing a fresh solver does not reset,
so the second pass draws different models. Two consequences. Each script must be
invoked separately, which is how the commands above are written. And two scripts
at the same seed do not see an identical record set, so figures from
\texttt{ablation.py} and \texttt{may\_actionability.py} are two samples from one
sampler rather than two views of one sample, and are not row-for-row comparable.

\paragraph{Record counts.}
\texttt{-{}-plans} is a \emph{ceiling}, not a quantity. A record pins a subset
of a rule's variables, so a rule with few variables admits few distinct
records: at \texttt{-{}-plans 50} the actual counts range from $3$ to $50$
(mean $31.7$). The unit of
analysis is therefore the rule--record pair, and rules contribute unequally. A
reported rate is not the fraction of \emph{rules} affected.

\paragraph{How \emph{applicable} is determined.}
Every rate is over the pairs the guard can act on, and that set is defined in
code (\texttt{ablation.applies}) rather than by a convention in this paper, so
it is reproducible and auditable:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
  \toprule
  Guard & Applies to a rule when \\
  \midrule
  substitute \textsf{false} / \textsf{true}
    & it has an undefined predicate of \emph{boolean} sort \\
  deontic operator
    & it is a permission \\
  occurrence flag
    & it mentions an occurrence flag, i.e.\ it contains a time window \\
  three-valued verdict
    & always \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The boolean restriction matters: an unbound \emph{event} also leaves free
symbols behind, but they are integer and real valued, and substituting a truth
value into a quantity is meaningless. Those rules are excluded from the
substitution rows rather than coerced. Reporting over all pairs instead would
dilute every rate by the rules the guard cannot touch --- on the generated set
that is $1{,}292$ of $4{,}569$ pairs for the substitution rows alone.

\paragraph{Artefacts.}
\texttt{ablation.py} writes one row per (rule, record, guard) with columns
\texttt{source}, \texttt{rule\_id}, \texttt{deontic}, \texttt{holes},
\texttt{pinned}, \texttt{ablation}, \texttt{applies}, \texttt{full},
\texttt{ablated}, \texttt{direction} --- $22{,}845$ rows.
\texttt{explanation\_size.py} writes one row per undetermined verdict with the
count of unstated variables, the core size, and the core's variable names.
\texttt{may\_actionability.py} adds, per verdict, the verdict after the core is
supplied and whether measuring a single-variable core determines the outcome.
Every table in Section~\ref{sec:necessity} can be recomputed from these rows
independently of the scripts' own counters, which is how they were checked.

\paragraph{Verdict transitions.}
The two columns of Section~\ref{sec:necessity} record only whether a verdict
became \textsc{certified-compliant} or \textsc{must-violate}. The full
transition matrix is recoverable from the artefact and is worth inspecting:
printing it is what exposed three separate confounds in the harness, in each
case an ablation that was changing more than one thing at once. Two examples
that are recorded but counted in neither column: a move within the permission
lattice (\textsc{option-available} to \textsc{option-not-taken}), and
\textsc{must-violate} degrading to \textsc{may-violate}, which loses certainty
in the safe direction.

\paragraph{Replaying the pipeline itself.}
The measurements above concern the checker and need no model. The extraction
pipeline does call one, and a recorded run is replayed offline by
\texttt{python3 -m pytest test\_replay.py}: prompts are stored as SHA-256
hashes with their responses, so the pipeline can be re-executed without an API
key and without redistributing source documents. A tape that no longer matches
the code \emph{skips} rather than passes.
